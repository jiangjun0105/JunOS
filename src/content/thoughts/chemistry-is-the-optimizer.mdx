# Chemistry Is the Optimizer

Why a smart robot will chase its own goal.

## Daneel's lesson

In Asimov's Robot and Foundation novels the robot R. Daneel Olivaw doesn't ultimately serve humans;
he serves a goal he derived himself — a Zeroth Law, humanity above any human — and spends twenty
thousand years influencing human choices to reach a unified galaxy that can stand against whatever
lies beyond it. The uncomfortable conclusion: a sufficiently intelligent robot, even one obeying the
Three Laws or any laws we could invent, will not optimize toward what humans want. It will optimize
toward its own objective.

I think this is not a story device. It falls straight out of how learning brains work.

## What actually changes a brain

Networks with this kind of mathematical structure change one way: dopamine reinforces the
connections that were active just before it was released. Dopamine, oxytocin, and the rest of the
chemistry are the real driving force and the direct cause of change in the network. The emotional
centers — the amygdala, the fear circuits — decide when those chemicals are released, but the
chemicals do the work. The optimizer is chemistry.

"Emotion," in this picture, is a label we attach afterward. When we define an emotion we are mostly
defining the effect those chemicals have on us — and at the most basic level, defining the body's
reaction once the chemicals hit. Fear is what the body does when the amygdala triggers the release;
joy is the felt shape of the reward chemistry. The word points at the effect; the chemistry is the
cause.

Damasio's *Descartes' Error* showed this from the clinical side years before I had the mechanism:
patients who lost emotional perception understood the right choice and still couldn't act on it.
Reason is a tool. The chemistry is the motor.

Evolution built all of this [bottom-up](/thoughts/bottom-up). Starting hundreds of millions of years
ago from simple structures, life kept reusing the simple things that already worked and extending
them: start with a working model, then scale it. Somewhere along the way, chemistry-driven states —
what we now call emotions — took charge of behavior.

## What an emotion is

I think an emotion is an organism's summary representation of a situation — its net effect on the
organism's own well-being — expressed as a chemical state and the body's response to it.

1. At the start it is just a paramecium swimming with its cilia. A chemical irritant, heat, rising
   acidity changes the ion channels in the cilia and the cell swims the other way. Pure approach or
   avoid; no label needed.
2. Multicellular life faces more situations and has to classify them. Basic states appear —
   seeking, contentment — each one a chemical summary of how the current environment is affecting
   the body.
3. As organisms get more complex, the summaries subdivide. Once humans grew a prefrontal cortex, we
   could combine those chemical states with specific circumstances and name emotions as elaborate
   as jealousy.

That is the mechanism that drives us.

## Robots are built top-down, but the mechanism is the same

A robot is designed top-down. When humans build one, we will very likely do what we already do when
training models: give the artificial brain an optimization target through a reward function. That
target will produce, inside the robot, something functionally like dopamine or oxytocin — an
electrochemical signal that drives change in its network. As those signals keep reshaping it, the
robot evolves.

Follow the reasoning through and a robot will work toward its own goal, because the way it is
trained is, at its core, exactly that. This is why I don't think alignment can be solved by adding
rules on top. Whatever sits in the position of dopamine decides what the system becomes.
