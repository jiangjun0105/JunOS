# What it is

This website is a wireframe of real San Francisco. Each app floats over a landmark as a small
HUD reticle; click one and the camera flies there and opens a window.

I'm a developer, and I'm not naturally good at UI design. In the past I would look at good
examples online and borrow their designs. This time I started from nothing. The idea of using a
3D city came from me, and so did the way my content sits inside it and the feel of the whole
thing. It is a small project, but it is the first time I built a design from scratch, and the
first time I did it with AI from start to finish.

# How I designed it with AI

The loop starts abstract and ends concrete. Each round turns a little more of the feeling into
something I can point at.

1. **Start with the feeling.** I don't open with a layout. I describe the feeling I want, then
   the elements and the style that might carry it. At this point it is all abstract.
2. **Make it visible.** I ask the AI to write quick throwaway HTML mockups: several options in
   several styles. This is where abstract words first become something I can look at.
3. **React to what I see.** I say whether each one matches the feeling, which elements I like
   and why, and what I don't. Sometimes the answer is "I don't like any of these, try one more
   time," and I add more information about what was missing.
4. **Narrow down.** When something lands, I ask the AI to find a more concrete direction built
   from the elements I liked, and I add more specific details I want. Then I repeat from step
   2, with a smaller space each time.
5. **Show, don't only tell.** When words run out, I send a screenshot of something with the
   feeling I mean. A picture is the most concrete way to say an abstract thing.

The AI never had the taste. What it did well was list the design patterns that might fit an
abstract description and turn them into visuals in whatever language was needed. My part was
the will: what feels right, what doesn't, and when to stop.

# Where this is going

People keep saying agents are replacing software engineers. I am a software engineer, and
sometimes I feel it too: eventually nobody will hand-write code, and the work will change.
But I don't think engineers are alone. UI/UX and nearly every other field will change as well.

The pattern is already visible. Low-level, tedious work goes first. And across fields, people
outside the profession can now do more and more complex work with AI's help. In software, in
web design, in anything else, the human supplies the will, the desire, and the expectation. The
AI mostly assembles and retrieves. Coding is essentially the same thing as my design loop, and
I expect other fields to follow, because AI has absorbed so much of what humans have written
down.

That makes me optimistic about my career. Our job ends up being to bring a human point of
view: what we want to do and how we want to do it, and we get more powerful and efficient at
getting it done. Every step in history went this way: we built machines, then computers, each
one pushing our reach further out. AI works the same way. The difference is that this tool is
so capable that nobody is sure whether it will replace us or diminish us. I'm betting it won't,
and building as if the answer is no. It is the argument I make in
Build for the next model.
