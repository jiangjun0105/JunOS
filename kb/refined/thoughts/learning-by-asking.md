I read fewer books lately. Most of what I learn now comes from talking with AI, and I think that is what learning will look like for most people.

# Two ways to learn

The first is to follow someone's output. A lecture, a course, a presentation: the learner walks the path the teacher laid out. The strength is structure — the teacher designed the syllabus in advance. The weakness is that the path rarely matches the learner's own way of understanding. A question comes up in the middle and the next step no longer connects, or the teacher moves on to something that isn't what you most wanted to know after the last part.

The second is to ask. This is the one I prefer. Picture standing on a blank white sheet of ignorance and exploring outward, widening the circle of what you understand. From the edge of that circle you can see the next ring of things you don't know, and you ask your way into them. The route is entirely yours, paced to your ability and pulled by your interest.

The first way is a teacher drawing a road on the white sheet for me — which I may not fill in well. The best version combines them: a teacher points the direction so I cover what needs covering, and I ask my way forward based on what I can understand and what I care about.

# How I read a paper

In my own research I always take the second road, even for a single paper. I ask AI what the paper is mainly about, to plant an anchor on the white sheet. Then I keep asking: why did they do it this way? What is the theory underneath? Where I need background I go deep, then come back to the paper's main line and look at the results. I absorb the authors' view without accepting it passively; the continuous asking and thinking is where my own new ideas come from.

This is how I learned most of what I know about neuroscience, and it worked far better than hunting for a ready-made course on YouTube. Courses are more systematic, but maybe 20% of one is what I actually care about or what would answer the question I have right now.

# No enclaves

The key to asking-based learning is that every new piece of knowledge attaches to the structure you already have. It extends the existing frame by one block instead of dropping an isolated island onto the white sheet. Islands disappear. Knowledge that grows on the existing skeleton is hard to forget, because it is connected to everything around it. Every answer you get by asking is connected by construction. A course video, which doesn't know what you already know, produces islands.

Each step this way is the easiest possible next thing to understand and the thing you most want to know right now — which is also why it feels intuitive. It suits anyone with their own appetite for knowing, and it suits an independent researcher like me especially well.
