# Why I train

I was a healthy kid. A car accident in middle school left me physically weak for a long time, and that is when I understood that health is not something you get to assume. I started training, and I have kept at it since.

For years I went to ordinary gyms, and eventually noticed that almost everything they teach is bodybuilding — the shape and size of individual muscles. That is not what a modern person needs, least of all someone who sits at a desk all day. What we need is capacity — stamina, strength you can use — and a body whose structure and posture are actually healthy.

# The lesson from one coach

The most useful coach I ever had spent our first session teaching me how to stand. His point: the body is bones, ligaments, and muscles connected into one system. It is not a collection of separate parts, and you cannot fix it one part at a time.

I got a personal demonstration of this recently. I have flat feet. Flat feet change how the foot bears weight, which rolls the ankle, which turns the lower leg outward, which rotates the pelvis, which tightens the glutes, tilts the pelvis forward, and finally shows up as lower-back pain. One tiny cause at the bottom of the chain — how the sole of the foot supports you — produces a whole-body cascade.

# What training should do

So the goal is not to make any one muscle big. It is to get every part of the chain assembled and connected the way it was designed, then build core strength so the whole thing is stable. That is also how you undo the occupational damage of desk work: the forward-tilted pelvis, the head pushed forward of the shoulders, the shoulders rolled in.

# Why this matters for people who think for a living

Our bodies haven't caught up with how modern life is lived. When a joint or muscle sits in the wrong position, the muscle is chronically over-stretched or compressed; that presses on the blood vessels and nerves running through it, slows circulation, and eventually produces pain and inflammation.

For a knowledge worker this is not a side issue. How well you work depends on how clear your head is, and a clear head depends on blood supply. Tight, misaligned muscles in the neck and shoulders reduce exactly that. Fixing posture is a productivity intervention, not a vanity one.

That is my small piece of hard-won understanding about health. I'm not a doctor; this is what worked for me and why I think it worked.
